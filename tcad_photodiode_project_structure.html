TCAD-Based Comparative Analysis of Semiconductor Photodiodes Using Si, Ge and GaAs

A structured project plan connecting TCAD simulation results with semiconductor material physics and optoelectronic applications.

1. Project Overview

The project investigates how semiconductor material selection affects the electrical and optical characteristics of a PIN photodiode. The same basic device concept is simulated using silicon (Si), germanium (Ge), and gallium arsenide (GaAs), followed by a comparative analysis.

Core research question: How does the choice of semiconductor material affect photodiode performance, and how can material properties such as bandgap and band structure explain the observed differences?

2. Device Structure

The recommended device is a PIN photodiode:

Incident Light ↓↓↓ ┌───────────────┐ │ P+ │ ├───────────────┤ │ │ │ I │ │ │ │ │ ├───────────────┤ │ N+ │ └───────────────┘

The lightly doped/intrinsic region provides an optical absorption and carrier-generation region. Geometry, doping strategy, contacts, and simulation conditions should be kept as comparable as reasonably possible across the three materials.

  1. Build the baseline PIN photodiode structure.
  2. Define geometry, mesh, doping profiles, materials and contacts.
  3. Choose appropriate semiconductor physics models.
  4. Run equilibrium and dark electrical simulations.
  5. Apply optical illumination and obtain illuminated characteristics.
  6. Sweep optical power and wavelength.
  7. Repeat the experiment for Si, Ge and GaAs.
  8. Extract comparable plots and device-physics quantities.

5. Planned Experiments

5.1 Dark I–V Characteristics

Plot current versus voltage without illumination. Compare reverse leakage, forward behavior, and other relevant junction characteristics.

5.2 Illuminated I–V Characteristics

Repeat the I–V simulation under different optical illumination levels and observe the increase in photogenerated current.

5.3 Photocurrent vs Optical Power

Study how photocurrent changes with incident optical power and evaluate linearity and sensitivity.

5.4 Responsivity vs Wavelength

This is one of the key experiments. Sweep wavelength and calculate responsivity:

R(λ) = Iph / Popt

Compare the spectral response of Si, Ge and GaAs and relate the cutoff behavior to their bandgaps.

5.5 Internal Device Physics

Extract TCAD distributions such as electric field, electron concentration, hole concentration, electrostatic potential, and—where supported reliably—generation and recombination rates.

5.6 Band Diagrams

Compare energy-band diagrams and use them to connect band structure and bandgap with optical absorption and carrier generation.

6. Expected Analysis Logic

Material properties ↓ Band structure / bandgap ↓ Photon absorption condition ↓ Electron–hole pair generation ↓ Photocurrent and responsivity ↓ Device performance ↓ Application suitability

The goal is not simply to report that the curves are different. Each observed difference should be linked to a physical property of the material or device.

7. Important GaAs / LED Connection

Important: The photodiode simulation should not by itself be presented as proof that GaAs is the best LED material. The LED conclusion should be a separate application/physics discussion, optionally supported by a small GaAs LED simulation later.

GaAs is a direct-bandgap semiconductor. In a light-emitting device, efficient radiative recombination is desirable:

electron + hole → photon

Direct-bandgap materials can support efficient optical transitions without the same momentum-conservation limitation associated with indirect-bandgap materials. This is a major reason GaAs is important in LEDs and semiconductor lasers.

Therefore, the project can use the photodiode study to establish the material-property differences, then connect those differences to the separate requirements of light detection and light emission.

10. Final Conclusion Direction

The project should conclude that there is no universally “best” semiconductor. Material selection depends on the target application and the interaction between bandgap, band structure, carrier properties, optical absorption, and device architecture.

  • Ge: its smaller bandgap makes it useful for longer-wavelength and infrared detection.
  • Si: combines mature semiconductor technology with strong electrical-device integration and useful photodetection capability.
  • GaAs: its direct bandgap and optical properties make it highly valuable for optoelectronic devices, including LEDs and lasers, while also providing useful photodetection behavior in its suitable wavelength range.

11. Recommended Project Story

The strongest presentation is:

Build one comparable PIN photodiode ↓ Swap Si → Ge → GaAs ↓ Run identical / controlled TCAD experiments ↓ Extract I–V, photocurrent, responsivity and internal physics ↓ Compare material-dependent behavior ↓ Explain differences using bandgap and band structure ↓ Connect material physics to real applications ↓ Optional extension: simulate a GaAs LED

This turns the work from “three TCAD simulations with screenshots” into a coherent device-physics investigation.
